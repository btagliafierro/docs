    Dambreak 3D¶

    This example demonstrates a 3-D dambreak simulation with an obstacle. A rectangular water column is initially held at rest, then suddenly released at time \(t=0\). The water collapses under gravity, flows through a rectangular tank, and impacts a building structure positioned downstream. The simulation captures the complex fluid dynamics including wave breaking, splashing, and fluid-structure interaction.

    For this dam break case, we use: - Fluid mk=0 for water particles - Boundary mk=0 for tank walls - Boundary mk=1 for building structure - Boundary mk=10 for a specific building wall (left side)

    Geometry Definition¶

    Domain Setup¶

    <definition dp="0.0085" units_comment="metres (m)"> 
    <pointmin x="-0.05" y="-0.05" z="-0.05" />
    <pointmax x="2" y="1" z="1" />
</definition>

    Particle spacing (dp): The inter-particle distance is 0.0085 m (8.5 mm). This is the fundamental resolution of the simulation. Smaller spacing provides better resolution but dramatically increases particle count and computational cost.

    Particle count estimation: For the water column (0.4 × 0.67 × 0.3 m), we get approximately (0.4/0.0085) × (0.67/0.0085) × (0.3/0.0085) ≈ 47 × 79 × 35 ≈ 130,000 fluid particles.

    Domain boundaries: The computational domain extends slightly beyond the physical geometry (from -0.05 to +2 m in x, -0.05 to +1 m in y and z). This buffer zone allows for proper boundary particle placement and prevents particles from exiting the domain prematurely.

    Shape Mode¶

    <setshapemode>dp | bound</setshapemode>
<setdrawmode mode="full" />

    Shape mode: The dp option means particles are placed at the inter-particle distance dp. The bound option indicates that boundary particles follow the same spacing rules as fluid particles.

    Draw mode: full mode creates complete solid or hollow objects with particles filling the entire volume or surface as specified.

    Fluid Block (Water Column)¶

    <setmkfluid mk="0" />
<drawbox>
    <boxfill>solid</boxfill>
    <point x="0" y="0" z="0" />
    <size x="0.4" y="0.67" z="0.3" />
</drawbox>

    Marker assignment: setmkfluid mk="0" designates all subsequent particles as fluid with marker 0. This allows the post-processing tools to identify and visualize water particles separately from boundaries.

    Solid fill: The water column is completely filled with particles arranged in a regular lattice at spacing dp.

    Position and size: The water column starts at the origin (0,0,0) and extends: - 0.4 m in the x-direction (length/downstream direction) - 0.67 m in the y-direction (width) - 0.3 m in the z-direction (height)

    Aspect ratio: The water column is relatively wide (0.67 m) compared to its length (0.4 m), creating a 3D dam break scenario rather than a 2D one. The height-to-length ratio of 0.3/0.4 = 0.75 is typical for dam break benchmarks.

    Tank Boundaries¶

    <setmkbound mk="0" />
<drawbox>
    <boxfill>bottom | left | right | front | back</boxfill>
    <point x="0" y="0" z="0" />
    <size x="1.6" y="0.67" z="0.4" />
</drawbox>
<shapeout file="Box"/>

    Boundary marker: setmkbound mk="0" designates these as boundary (solid wall) particles with marker 0.

    Selective filling: The boxfill parameter specifies which faces to create: - bottom: Floor of the tank (z=0 plane) - left/right: Side walls parallel to x-z plane (y=0 and y=0.67) - front/back: Side walls parallel to y-z plane (x=0 and x=1.6) - No top: Allows water to splash upward without ceiling obstruction

    Tank dimensions: The tank extends 1.6 m in length (4× the water column length), providing sufficient space for the water to propagate and interact with the obstacle. The width matches the water column (0.67 m), creating a channel-like flow.

    Shape export: shapeout file="Box" saves this geometry to a separate file for visualization and verification before running the simulation.

    Obstacle (Building)¶

    The building is created in three steps: void creation, external walls, and marker assignment for specific surfaces.

    Step 1: Create Void¶

    <setmkvoid />
<drawbox>
    <boxfill>solid</boxfill>
    <point x="0.9" y="0.24" z="0" />
    <size x="0.12" y="0.12" z="0.45" />
</drawbox>

    Void marker: setmkvoid removes any particles in this region. This creates the hollow interior of the building, ensuring no fluid or boundary particles exist inside.

    Position: The building is placed at x=0.9 m, which is: - 2.25× the initial water column length downstream - Centered in the y-direction: y=0.24 m, which is (0.67-0.12)/2 ≈ 0.275 m from the left wall (approximately centered)

    Building size: 0.12 m × 0.12 m × 0.45 m - Square footprint (0.12 × 0.12 m) - Height of 0.45 m, which is 1.5× the initial water height (0.3 m), ensuring the building protrudes above the water level

    Step 2: External Walls¶

    <setmkbound mk="1" />
<drawbox>
    <boxfill>top | left | right | front | back</boxfill>
    <point x="0.9" y="0.24" z="0" />
    <size x="0.12" y="0.12" z="0.45" />
</drawbox>

    Building marker: mk="1" distinguishes the building from the tank walls (mk="0"), allowing separate force calculations and analysis of building loads.

    Wall surfaces: Creates boundary particles on five faces: - top: Roof of the building (z=0.45 plane) - left/right: Side walls parallel to x-z plane - front/back: Side walls parallel to y-z plane
    - No bottom: The building sits directly on the tank floor (which already has boundary particles from the tank definition)

    Physical interpretation: This creates a hollow box structure that water will flow around and potentially overtop.

    Step 3: Special Marker for Impact Surface¶

    <setmkbound mk="10" />
<drawbox>
    <boxfill>left</boxfill>
    <point x="0.9" y="0.24" z="0" />
    <size x="0.12" y="0.12" z="0.45" />
</drawbox>
<shapeout file="Building"/>

    Impact surface marker: The left face (facing upstream, where water first impacts) is reassigned marker mk="10". This allows post-processing tools to specifically calculate impact forces, pressure distribution, and loads on this critical surface.

    Why separate markers?: In experimental validations, pressure gauges are typically placed on the impact face. Using mk="10" allows direct comparison between simulation and experimental pressure measurements on this surface.

    Shape export: Saves the building geometry separately for verification.

    7.1.1 DAMBREAK

    A 2-D dam break can be executed using wCaseDambreakVal2D_win64_GPU.bat (xCaseDambreakVal2D_linux64_GPU.sh in linux), which is the file that summarises the tasks to be carried out using the different codes in a given order:

    Figure 7-2 shows different instants of the CaseDambreakVal2D simulation. The left frames show the PartFluid_xxxx.vtk (computed with PartVTK) and the right ones show the Slices_xxxx.vtk (obtained with IsoSurface)

    Figure 7-2. Instants of the CaseDambreakVal2D simulation. Colour represents velocity of the particles.

    With this example, the experimental data of [Koshizuka and Oka, 1996] can be numerically reproduced. The file EXP_X-DamTipPosition_Koshizula&Oka1996.txt includes the X-position of the tip of the dam for several instants (before the impact with the other wall).

    Numerical gauges (<gauges>) allows the user to compute different magnitudes during the execution of DualSPHysics. In this case, two gauges were created at x=0.2 to compute the water column height evolution and at z=0.03 to compute the X-position of the tip of the dam. The gauges can be also observed in the right panel of Figure 7-2.

    The 3-D test case consists of a dam-break flow impacting with a structure inside a numerical tank. No moving boundary particles are involved in this simulation and no movement is defined. This case is executed using wCaseDambreak_win64_GPU.bat (xCaseDambreak_linux64_GPU.sh in linux),

    Different instants of the CaseDambreak simulation are shown in Figure 7-3 where the PartFluid_xxxx.vtk of the fluid particles, the CaseDambreak_Box_Dp.vtk and CaseDambreak_Building_Dp.vtk are depicted.

    Figure 7-3. Instants of the CaseDambreak simulation visualized using Paraview. Colour represents velocity of the particles.

    Using MeasureTool code, numerical velocities have been computed in the list of points described in the file CaseDambreak_PointsVelocity.txt, which are (0.754, 0.31, 0.02), (0.754, 0.31, 0.04) and (0.754, 0.31, 0.06) using information of fluid particles within the kernel interaction. Numerical values of Vx-velocity in the x-direction are shown in Figure 7-4 for these three points.

    Figure 7-4. Numerical velocity computed at three different locations.

    Pressure values are also computed using MeasureTool in the list of points described in the file CaseDambreak_PointsPressure.txt, which corresponds to locations in the front wall of the structure (Figure 7-5) using information of fluid particles within the kernel interaction. Due to the use of the dynamic boundaries, probes used for measuring quantities should not be placed at the exact position of boundary particles. The forces exerted by the boundary particles create a small gap between them and fluid particles (1.5 times h); as a result, probes placed inside the gap will have a reduced fluid particle population and will produce either an incorrect or no result. To avoid this issue, it is proposed that probes are placed at a distance 1.5h from the boundary positions.

    In this example, the PointsPressure_Incorrect probes are placed at the exact position as the boundary particles at the building, while the PointsPressure_Correct probes are placed at a distance of 1.5_h_ (for the default conditions and parameters of the case) from the boundaries. The first probes produce no pressure results as the number of fluid particles for the interpolation is too small, while the second shows the pressure impact by the water flow.

    Figure 7-5. Position of incorrect (red) and correct (green) pressure probes in CaseDambreak.

    Note that the probes are not automatically associated with the smoothing length h and their position has to be changed manually if the resolution changes. Also, note that by increasing resolution (i.e. using a smaller “dp”) the gap is reduced.

    Figure 7-6 shows the pressure computed at the correct numerical probes.

    Figure 7-6. Pressure values computed at different locations in CaseDambreak.

    This case also shows how to compute forces exerted against a structure. In this way the force is computed using ComputeForces as the summation of the acceleration values of the boundary particles that form the front wall of the structure (mk=21) multiplied by mass. Figure 7-7 shows the time series of the numerical force.

    Figure 7-7. Force (X-component) exerted on the front wall of the structure in CaseDambreak.

    In fact, the methodology outlined here was used in for the validation of DualSPHysics presented in [Crespo et al. (2011)].
